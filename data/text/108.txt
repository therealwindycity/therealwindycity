Cheyenne advances construction regulations, officially removes noise rules ahead of final vote
by
Garrett Grochowski

CHEYENNE, Wyo. — The Cheyenne City Council voted Monday night to advance an ordinance establishing new local construction project rules. The item will be back before the council for adoption in two weeks, officially without a section enforcing noise limits.
During its meeting, the council also discussed a series of ordinances that updated the city’s Unified Development Code, the rulebook for city zoning and land use, in different ways. The council adopted on third reading an ordinance to establish agriculture as a viable land use and set some new rules for owning animals on properties in the city.
Up for its second Cheyenne City Council reading was an ordinance establishing new rules for construction sites. Requirements include separation of labor housing from residential areas by at least 1,000 feet, nuisance mitigation features such as downward-facing lighting, and more. After weeks of deliberation by the council and its public services committee, it’s taken a different form than how it first appeared. Notably, a section enforcing strict decibel noise limits was removed via a substitute ordinance, much to the disappointment of some attendees.
As it was originally drafted, amendments to the UDC mandated that, as recorded at the property line, a construction site would not have been able to produce more than 65 decibels of constant noise during the day and 60 at night in non-residential areas and 45 decibels during the day and 40 decibels at night near homes.
Cheyenne residents who have been fighting for tighter regulations on industrial projects like data centers lauded the suggestion, which followed the World Health Organization’s suggested guidelines for healthy noise limits, saying that noise produced by construction projects is bad for human health.
However, the Public Services Committee moved to omit the noise limits from the ordinance
, keeping the rest of the proposed regulations intact. They introduced and supported a substitute amendment for City Council consideration that stipulated instead that data center uses must produce no discernible noise at the property line or else be classified and regulated as an industrial use, which limits what type of zone districts they can operate in.
The change, councilors in favor of it argued, doesn’t force the city to enforce a burdensome regulation and maintains expectations that the city has set for industrial operators in recent years.
Councilor Michelle Aldrich noted that the specific limits required by the original ordinance were harsh.
“A standard dishwasher or a quiet vacuum cleaner runs around 65 decibels. A clothes dryer is between 60 and 65. A commercial office printer, a laser printer, an electric toothbrush is around 60–65 decibels,” she said. “So when I asked about a 40-decibel piece of equipment example, I get a desktop computer — that’s 35–40; an air purifier — that’s 35–40. I think that we have a hard time wrapping our heads around what the decibel level is for when we talk about ‘just a 60 or a 65 or a 40 or a 45.'”
The amendments also apply to all construction sites, not just data centers, she said, meaning the city could be shooting itself in the foot over-regulating construction sites when most commenters are in opposition solely to data centers.
Councilors in favor of retaining the noise standards as they were originally proposed noted that decibels are measured logarithmically, meaning the difference between 45 decibels and 55 decibels is greater than it appears.
“At night, when ambient background noise drops, a consistent 55-decibel mechanical hum from a data center is intrusive,” Councilor Lawrence Wolfe argued.
According to a
Decibel Level Comparison Chart published by Yale Environmental Health and Safety
, hearing loss becomes a concern with sustained adjusted decibel levels over 80 — equivalent to a dial tone or busy traffic. A
similar chart from the American Academy of Audiology
places the threshold for hearing damage slightly higher, recommending hearing protection for sounds at or above 85 decibels. There is
very limited scientific evidence
backing up additional claims that vibrations produced by data center noise can harm humans.
The council ultimately approved the substitute and advanced the ordinance, setting it up for final adoption in two weeks. It also advanced an ordinance specifically regulating data centers, mandating Special Use review for on-site power plants, fuel cells or wind turbines, preventing data centers from installing continuous power generation by right.
Ultimately, Mark Rinne, one of the ordinance’s loudest sponsors, said the changes will go a long way in giving the city real power in enforcing best-practice construction standards and punishing noncompliant developers.
“We’ve sat up here for months and listened to concerns of the public and and I think we’re addressing those concerns, but it needs to be codified,” Rinne said. “Currently what we’re doing is policy, and if there’s a violation of policy there’s really no punishment for it. If it’s actually an ordinance and we have it spelled out, ‘This is what’s required,’ and one of the data centers does not follow what’s required, then we have some recourse.”
The city may have to “tweak the noise,” he said, but making rules now means that the city maintains local control over data centers — which he noted the state was also looking to regulate soon — and make the community a more pleasant place to live.
