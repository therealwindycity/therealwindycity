Wyoming education chief ‘baffled’ governor moved to deny school-choice funding
By
Katie Klingsporn
,
Wyofile
Wyoming Superintendent of Public Instruction Megan Degenfelder is “baffled” by Gov. Mark Gordon’s move to deny funding for the state’s embattled school choice program in 2027, she told WyoFile.
Gordon recently released his supplemental budget, which seeks additional funding after the Legislature approved a two-year budget in March.
In his budget book,
he recommended funding $0 of the $32.5 million the Wyoming Department of Education requested to operate the Steamboat Legacy Program for 2027-28.
It’s the latest blow to the universal school voucher program, which the
Wyoming Legislature passed in 2025
. Through the program,
Wyoming residents can apply to receive up to $7,000
in state money per student to pay for costs associated with private K-12 education. Students must be disenrolled from public school to qualify.
The program has been dogged by questions about its constitutionality since its inception. A 2025 lawsuit challenging the program delayed its implementation one year, but more than 4,000 students enrolled this fall. The court has yet to determine whether the program violates Wyoming’s constitution.
That ongoing litigation was a major factor in Gordon’s decision to deny the funding request, he said in a statement to WyoFile.
Now, he added, “the funding of this program is rightly in the hands of the Legislature, and they can decide, as they did last year, if they wish to fund the program now or wait for the ruling of the courts.”
For Degenfelder, who has championed the school-choice program, Gordon’s decision was surprising, “especially given the Governor’s
celebratory signing of the act.”
Gordon called it a “remarkable achievement for Wyoming” when he signed into law in March 2025.
“Over 4,200 students enrolled in the program once again have their education disrupted by partisan politics, through no fault of their own,” Degenfelder said.
As Gordon indicated, the funding issue will now go to the Wyoming Legislature. Once the governor finalizes his request, it moves through the statehouse during the legislative session. The Joint Appropriations Committee will begin holding budget sessions in January.
Meanwhile, the court case continues with no clear end in sight. On Monday, the state asked the Wyoming Supreme Court to weigh in anew.
Laramie County District Court Judge Peter Froelicher
refused in early November to dismiss
the lawsuit against the state of Wyoming, Degenfelder and Treasurer Curt Meier. In the latest filing, defendants asked the Supreme Court to review that order.
A rocky path
The Steamboat Legacy Scholarship Act significantly expanded school choice in Wyoming by replacing an income-qualified 2024 “education savings account” program. The 2025 law passed amid objections it would violate the Wyoming Constitution, which bars the state from handing out public funds to individuals, corporations or associations “except for necessary support of the poor.”
When the state opened Steamboat Legacy Scholarship applications in 2025, nearly 4,000 applicants signed up to receive the money for the 2025-26 school year. Then in June 2025, a group of parents and the Wyoming Education Association
sued to halt the program
.
The lawsuit set off a flurry of motions and hearings. Asserting the program would cause irreparable harm to plaintiffs, the lawsuit asked the court to block the voucher program while the case proceeds. Defendants, meanwhile, asked that the case be dismissed.
Froelicher granted that plaintiff’s preliminary injunction request in July 2025, barring the state from releasing any money while the case was litigated.
The state, however, fought his injunction, and further challenges led to the Supreme Court taking up the matter. The high court in May reversed the injunction, concluding the group of parents challenging the state program “failed to clearly show they might be irreparably and personally injured.”
The Supreme Court determination that plaintiffs failed to make a clear showing of such possible injury spurred defendants to ask Froelicher once more for dismissal. For the second time, he rejected the request, moving the case closer to a trial.
In the latest filing, defendants asked the Supreme Court to review and reverse Froelicher’s November order.
Constitutional questions
The lawsuit argued the voucher program violates the Wyoming Constitution, which makes public education a paramount state commitment. The constitution’s education clause states, “the legislature shall provide for the establishment and maintenance of a complete and uniform system of public instruction.”
A previous Wyoming Supreme Court ruling on education funding “found that … ‘all aspects of the school finance system are subject to strict scrutiny,’ and that ‘any state action interfering with [the right to equal educational opportunity] must be closely examined before it can be said to pass constitutional muster,’” the current lawsuit states.
This voucher program, it asserted, does not pass that muster. That’s because “the state cannot circumvent those requirements by funding private education that is not uniform and that meets none of the required state constitutional standards for education.”
The program will draw students away from public schools, the Wyoming Education Association also asserts, and shrinking enrollment leads to smaller state budget apportionment. Critics
have also questioned
whether the program aligns with constitutional language prohibiting public school funds to be used to assist private or parochial schools.
Defendants, however, argue that plaintiffs lack standing because they have failed to show irreparable harm posed by the program. More broadly, supporters of the program say it provides parents with adequate options for finding the best learning opportunities for their children.
The ultimate result could prove disruptive for the
families that have enrolled their children
for costs related to everything from tutoring to online classes, supplies and private school tuition.
After the Supreme Court ended the injunction in May, the state relaunched the program for the current school year. Again, several thousand applications were submitted.
In a Nov. 20 post on X, the Wyoming Freedom Caucus alluded to the fact that pressure on Wyoming legislators can influence the Steamboat Legacy Scholarship’s fate.
“If you’re among the 4,500 families who opted into WY’s universal school choice program, you likely just learned that Gordon’s supplemental budget would DEFUND it,” the post read. “Meanwhile, the budget crams $100M into the state’s savings account. What will the 69th Legislature do?”
