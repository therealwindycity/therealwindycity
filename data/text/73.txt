Cheyenne committee carves out noise standards from construction regulations overhaul
CHEYENNE, Wyo. — A week after the Cheyenne City Council postponed a proposed update to city code setting stricter rules for large construction sites, a city committee is removing a section that would have established specific noise limits, instead hoping that a future council can someday set better, more scientifically based standards.
Last week, the Cheyenne City Council considered an ordinance amending the city’s Unified Development Code to establish a series of rules and expectations for construction sites. The changes, if passed, would be comprehensive. The ordinance would require construction sites to establish setbacks from nearby neighborhoods, keep temporary workforce housing 1,000 feet or more away from schools and churches, forbid city officials from signing NDAs with developers and more.
Before Monday, it also established stringent noise limits for construction sites.
Under the proposed changes, large industrial sites would have had to adhere to strict noise standards. As recorded at the property line, a site would not have been able to produce more than 65 decibels of constant noise during the day and 60 at night in non-residential areas and 45 decibels during the day and 40 decibels at night near homes.
That standard, construction and data center company representatives told the Cheyenne City Council last week, was a step too far.
“To bring that down to a lower level — excuse the term — but it’s ludicrous,” Paul Steed, project manager at local contractor Mechanical Systems, told the council.
He said that a change of that magnitude, while intended to limit concerns Cheyenne residents have about living near data centers, puts all local businesses — especially construction companies — in danger.
“It would greatly limit my livelihood,” he said. “There would have to be measures taken that would be very expensive to develop in Cheyenne, particularly subdivisions next to residential. I’ll have to put up sound walls or whatever else you’d have to do to limit the noise that reaches local residents.”
Representatives from multiple data centers as well as Cheyenne LEADS, the economic development firm that has facilitated the city’s recent industrial growth, also spoke against raising noise standards last Monday. They argued that the World Health Organization noise guidelines that the proposed limits follow are supposed to be measured from where people may actually be listening from rather than at property lines and that enforcing stricter noise limits may put areas in Cheyenne that people wouldn’t have expected in violation of city code.
“If true, that would present significant enforcement challenges for the city and have severe unintended consequences,” Chris Tetlow, Microsoft’s Cheyenne metro director, told the council.
Their concerns were taken into consideration by the council, which elected to postpone consideration of the ordinance for a week. That sent it back to the Public Services Committee, which workshops ordinances like the one in consideration, to give councilors more time to work out the kinks. Ultimately, the committee voted to support and advance the ordinance, but with the amendment to the noise standards removed.
Before councilors could make the decision, they heard more public testimony, including opinions both in support of and against the stricter noise standards. By the time the committee needed to take a vote, both members of the public and councilors on the committee themselves said this was one of the most confusing conversations they had ever had in the City Council chambers.
“If I were going to send a text on this, it would say ‘WTH.’ What the heck; my goodness,” Public Services Committee Chair Michelle Aldrich said.
That sentiment was echoed by legal representatives from Related Digital, one of Cheyenne’s new data center companies. Hathaway and Kunz laywer Kristen Lee said that, besides the process being confusing, Related Digital’s position is that it agreed to follow specific standards when their project was approved and it still believes those standards are thorough enough.
“I’m hesitating because I hate to admit this, but I’m having a tough time following the discussion,” Lee told the committee. “We’ve been pretty diligent at our office to make charts of what’s been proposed, of what it is and where we’re headed. I really am, I’m embarrassed to say I can’t just come before you today with a crystallized — I know our position. Our position is please, we built to the specifications of the lot and we were approved and we had our sound engineers that we hired — experts in the country — go out [and spend] time on our site.”
Despite the widespread confusion, Lee and her fellow representatives’ position will win out for now. Public comment from both proponents and opponents of the noise standard in the last few weeks was taken into consideration by the city, compelling planning staff to suggest passing an amendment carving the issue out and setting it aside for a later time. That would, Councilor Kathy Emmons said, give the council the ability to pass the other critical and time-sensitive regulations included in the ordinance, but allow the city more time to study noise as a separate issue.
“We’ve got to get this right,” Emmons said. “There’s a lot of affected parties on both sides of the equation. There’s a lot to do with the sound issues that we need to make sure we get right, so what I would like to do is to set that aside so that we can have a bigger conversation and make sure that we have a good process for doing this.”
“It’s got to be an organized, coordinated approach, but at the same time allow us to go forward with the protections around water, sewer, surface discharge, electricity, natural gas, lighting, public records, fire and safety — all the things that we’ve heard people say in the last however many months we’ve been doing this that they want to have regulations about and we want to have regulations about.”
The committee and staff agreed that a council workshop in the future would be the best way to get a new conversation specifically dedicated to noise going.
However, Councilor Pete Laybourn said he can see where the conversation is going. The “fix it later” approach is easy to take until it’s actually time to fix it, he argued.
“Who’s going to do it? When are they going to do it? How are they going to do it? What is our involvement going to be?” Laybourn said. “That’s really my question.”
He added that carving out the noise regulations rather than postponing the ordinance altogether, given the pace of change Cheyenne has already experienced in the last two years, would leave industrial developers too much time to leave a permanent mark on the community. Not only does a delay set the city even further back in catching up to data center and other industrial project regulations, he continued, but also the way by which the city is doing so — through a “cascade of substitutes,” he said — is making it impossible for Cheyenne residents to follow. He voted against leaving noise limits out of the final ordinance.
Ultimately, though, the committee substituted by amendment a carve-out of the noise regulations, advancing the ordinance to the Cheyenne City Council for consideration next week. With questions lingering as to how the city should regulate sound produced by large construction sites, a future work session and subsequent code amendment could be in the works.
For now, the city will continue enforcing noise standards circumstantially, which Councilor Mark Rinne said makes sense to him given the variability in where construction projects are located, what they are adjacent to and how many people they affect.
“The one thing that seems to be most controversial in the very end, as we’re all learning, is the noise, and at least this is addressing the noise on a case-by-case basis as we’ve been doing it,” he said.
