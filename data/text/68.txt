Cheyenne Frontier FFA team earns top awards during national convention

Dan Cepeda

Cheyenne Frontier FFA members Samantha Duran, Brody Medina, and Emily Shuck, are seen after their win during the 99th National FFA Convention & Expo in Indianapolis in October, 2026. (Courtesy FFA)
CHEYENNE, Wyo. –– Three Cheyenne Frontier FFA members returned home with accolades and lifetime memories after attending the 99th National FFA Convention & Expo in Indianapolis, Indiana, late last month.
Brody Medina, 18, now a student at the University of Wyoming, along with Cheyenne East High School students Samantha Duran, 15, and Emily Shuck, 17, were each recognized for their projects at the national convention. Medina graduated from Cheyenne East in time to be eligible for this year’s convention, he said.
Medina was honored with the 2026 Agricultural Communications Proficiency Award, while Duran and Shuck each won the Agriscience Social Science Award.
According to a release from the FFA, Duran and Shuck researched how demographics influence acceptance of agricultural marketing on social media. “They used a survey to collect data and results concluded that social media is a valuable marketing tool for people in smaller city, populations, younger individuals, and males,” said the release.
Medina served as the first communications intern for the Wyoming FFA, as well as social media coordinator for Frontier Livestock. He has also published more than 23 articles in various Wyoming and national ag-focused publications
In addition to competing for his award, Medina was able to coach Duran and Shuck on their projects. “I’m so incredibly proud of the success that our chapter saw at nationals,” he said in a phone interview. Medina started in FFA as a freshman in high school and competed in proficiency awards in the past.
Medina is currently studying secondary agriculture education at UW, he said. “The reason for that was my foundation in FFA, and I gained a passion for ag industry and for teaching the next generation about the production of food, fiber, and natural resources, so my career goal is very tied to my experience in FFA,” he said.
Duran said her family has a history with FFA, which encouraged her to join the club and start working in agriscience.
Shuck had started competing in FFA in middle school in other projects. “Agriscience was kind of a newer thing to me, so I just decided to take that extra step in my case and do two projects, and that opened up the opportunity to compete in state and bring us to nationals,” she said. “I’m thinking of going into agricultural law right now; it’s extremely interesting.”
Duran, the youngest of the group, is still considering her post-high school options. “The main one I want to go into is ag ed, and that’s because I’m really passionate about FFA and I enjoy teaching other people about it,” she said. Her other interest is range management.
Shuck and Duran said they had put in some 400 hours working on their project in preparation for the convention. “We definitely spent a lot of our time doing this, hours upon hours,” said Duran.
Medina spent some 1,500 hours working on ag communications for his proficiency in agricultural communications entrepreneurship placement, and then another 700 hours on the agriscience research project.
All of them presented live in front of audiences and a livestream during the convention. “For me, it was pretty nerve-wracking, especially while we were sitting down [backstage] waiting to be called,” Duran said. “I wasn’t talking to anybody, and that’s rare for me, but it was a great experience afterwards when all the butterflies wore off and we could just enjoy the moment.”
“Sam [Duran] and I are kind of polar opposites when it comes to this,” said Shuck, who tends to be comfortable while waiting to present. For her, the nerves kick in when the presentation starts. “It was kind of an unreal feeling,” she said. “That describes the whole trip. It’s kind of unreal to recognize that we’re top 10 in the nation, and then after we won, we’re first in the nation.”
Medina described the team as a whole as passionate about the work. “Whether it’s research or communications, these are things we would have done regardless of the recognition.”
When many think of FFA, they think of ranch projects. However, the Frontier team is keen to promote FFA’s much broader purpose.
“We always say, it’s not just cows, plows and sows,” Medina said. “It’s not all 100% connected to agriculture, but FFA is an incredible organization, casting a wide net and giving opportunities to students across the sector of agriculture, not just specifically in production or those traditional fields.”
