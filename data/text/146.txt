Controversial railroad ties set to be removed from Laramie’s West Side
Rep. Karlee Provenza says Omaha Track will move the ties out of state starting Monday.

LARAMIE—The piles of creosote-soaked railroad ties that raised public health and safety concerns among West Side residents and local officials starting this summer will soon be moved elsewhere, according to Democratic Rep. Karlee Provenza.
“I got a phone call from the legal counsel for Omaha Track, who oversees the storage of the ties, and they told me last night that they were moving the tracks out of the area starting on Monday,” Provenza told WyoFile on Friday.
Union Pacific began stacking the ties this summer on its property adjacent to the Laramie River and the West Side, a historic, working-class neighborhood sandwiched between the train tracks and Interstate 80.
As
WyoFile previously reported
, neighboring residents say the pungent smell of the creosote — a preservative long used to protect wooden ties from natural decay and pests — quickly fouled up the area.
The preservative is a mixture of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons “derived from the distillation of tar from wood or coal,” according to the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency. Depending on exposure levels, it poses health risks, including cancer, and can linger in soils and pollute water.
Provenza, whose House district encompasses the West Side, said it’s her understanding that the ties are being moved out of state, but those details remain unclear. Omaha Track and its legal counsel did not respond to WyoFile’s request by publishing time.
“I won’t celebrate until the ties are gone, and I hope they’re not being sent to someone else’s backyard,” she said. “But it’s a big deal that the people that live at the end of Cedar Street and near that area aren’t going to be breathing in creosote and aren’t going to have to worry about their homes burning down.”
Martha Jenkins sits on her front porch in Laramie’s West Side. (Maggie Mullen/WyoFile)
Soon after the ties appeared, a handful of West Side residents formed a nonprofit, Healthy Neighborhoods, and threatened to sue Union Pacific and Omaha Track if the companies didn’t remove the ties. They felt legal action might be necessary after initial responses from local and state authorities were tepid.
Martha and Mark Jenkins, two of the nonprofit’s founders, told WyoFile on Friday they were “delighted” at the news.
“To prevail in this kind of local battle, I think it’s very meaningful,” Mark said. “I think it shows that people can pull together if they feel like something is actually wrong and make a difference for each other.”
