Johnson County residents talk land use
BUFFALO — Johnson County residents discussed what they want for the future of county land use at the Comprehensive Land Use Plan public hearings over two nights the week of Nov. 16. Between 10 and 20 residents attended each meeting, and those who commented generally agreed that while regulations are unpopular, the county needs to take a more proactive approach to land-use planning.
“Listening to all of you, it sounds like we’re very much at a point that we must be proactive (about land use) because otherwise we’re reactive, and then the cows are out, the gate’s not closed in time, and they’ve scattered,” Julie Baker, chair of the Johnson County Planning and Zoning Commission, said during the Kaycee meeting.
The Planning and Zoning Commission scheduled two public hearings — one in Kaycee and one in Buffalo — to discuss the draft 2026 Johnson County Comprehensive Land Use Plan. The document’s purpose is to serve as a non-regulatory guide for future land management decisions by Johnson County government bodies, to guide development in the county in a way that makes sense for the community. The document must also be considered when the federal government proposes projects in the county.
The draft is an update to the 2005 Comprehensive Land Use Plan, which was based on data, industry and social trends from 2000. The 2026 version is based on data from 2022 and surveys of county residents to ensure that their views are represented.
At both meetings, the residents in attendance had little to say about the draft itself, with the few comments calling it a representative document that should guide decision-making.
“I fully support the land use plan that you’ve written. I think it’s right on the mark and getting us where we need to go,” Buffalo resident Bob McDowell said.
But the conversations quickly turned to the future of land use in the county and what the county wants to protect.
Crossroads
There’s a data center and wind-and-solar industrial boom occurring in Wyoming, and a 4,000-acre solar farm was proposed in Johnson County just last week.
County Planner Jim Waller set the stakes for what the county is facing.
“We have a target on ourselves. What is really hard is we’re the ninth largest in the area, and we’re the 50th smallest in population. It’s like we have a big target,” Waller said at the Kaycee meeting.
To that, the public voiced their concerns over losing agricultural production land, water use, excessive subdivisions and ensuring the landscape that drew many of them here remains intact for future generations.
Many public comments called for some form of proactivity and enforceable measures to address concerns.
“We have, and we live in a beautiful place. We just need to protect it. I’d like to draw the drawbridge up too, but we can’t. So we have to think ahead,” Johnson County resident Bob Ruby said in Kaycee. “It’s hard to make the call, I’m not saying we messed up, but we realize we got a problem. So sometimes you’ve got to draw a line and say, as painful as it is, we’re going to try and protect what we have.”
County resident Margaret Smith said in Buffalo that she’s concerned about “unintended consequences of not planning, not having something in a place where you cannot fix the problem that minute, and it’s done.”
And Peter Camino offered comments at the Buffalo meeting.
“This land use plan needs more teeth in it. … The entire county needs to take a hold of this thing and put some teeth in it,” he said.
County Commissioner Jeff Shelley at the Kaycee meeting explained that because Johnson County doesn’t have land use regulations, it is up to the state to permit large industrial sites and the county doesn’t get much of a say.
“I don’t like the word ‘zoning,’ I don’t like regulation, but I do think for our future and our kids’ futures that we do need to look at a few more industrial regulations, kind of in that area,” Shelley said. He later added that it’s “such a fine line that we have to look at. And this is why I want these conversations, and I hope we have a lot more of them.”
The state does have regulations, and there were those concerned about the county adding another layer of government.
Johnson County resident John Hanson said in Buffalo he doesn’t want the plans to turn into regulations, the state’s regulations are sufficient and that once regulations are created, they are rarely overturned.
“As regulations grow, freedoms die,” Hanson said.
And at the Kaycee meeting, county resident Wade Curuchet called for a cautious approach.
“They (other counties) had a really good commission of commissioners at the time, and they started out with these bare-bones regulations, and this stuff, as commissioners and the planning and zoning committee went on and off, this stuff got blown way out of proportion,” Curuchet said.
Waller, at the Buffalo meeting, said that a land use regulation is not the only way to address people’s concerns.
“There are other things that the county can do to help mitigate, develop and direct development as it does occur,” Waller said. “We do have existing regulations, and some of the regulations can be tailored to address concerns with water.”
The planning and zoning commission will vote soon on the comprehensive land use plan draft, which then needs to be approved by the county commissioners. Once adopted, the planning and zoning board will hold workshops to discuss whether additional land use rules are needed.
Without countywide zoning, property owners generally have greater latitude to use their land as they see fit. But that freedom also restricts the county’s ability to review and probe new industrial developments of any size.
Planning and zoning commission board member Travis Pearson summarized the county’s crossroads.
“Do you trust your neighbors to do the right thing and keep it rural? Or do you think they’ll sell out, when somebody starts showing up with checks that have got so many zeros they can’t say no. If we trust them, then this (no regulation) is right,” Pearson said at the Kaycee meeting. “If we don’t, it’s time to ask what will I give up to keep my neighbor from imposing something that will change the character of the community.”
