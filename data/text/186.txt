Marketplace insurance premiums to rise again
BUFFALO — For self-employed contractor Scott Madsen, choosing to go without health insurance is a calculated risk. Madsen, who is in his 50s, said as someone who is fairly healthy and rarely needs to visit a doctor, buying health insurance just doesn’t make financial sense.
“I haven’t even really tried to get insurance the past couple years,” Madsen said. “Between premiums and copays and deductibles, I’d have to spend well over $20,000 before insurance would kick in and pay anything worthwhile.”
Madsen is one of a growing number of Wyomingites who are choosing to forgo health insurance in the face of rising premiums.
The Affordable Care Act Marketplace, created under the Affordable Care Act of 2010, allows people who don’t have access to health insurance through an employer or a government program to purchase individual coverage. Plans are offered by private insurance companies, and eligible households can receive federal premium tax credits to offset the cost of monthly premiums.
The amount of financial assistance depends largely on household income, while premiums vary based on factors such as age, location and the level of coverage selected.
But even with federal assistance, rising premiums can make coverage unaffordable for some households.
After a 24% average rate increase for the 2026 plan year, this year Wyomingites buying insurance through the individual marketplace can expect to see an average premium increase of 11%, according to the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services.
Blue Cross Blue Shield of Wyoming rates will increase 7.51% to 11.43% depending on the plan selected. United Health Care plans will increase 22.29% to 26.24%.
In its rate increase filing request, Blue Cross Blue Shield wrote that the primary drivers of the rate increase are the escalating costs of medical care and prescription drugs and the expiration of enhanced federal ACA tax credits at the end of 2025, which led to enrollment drops. Those enrollment drops led to sicker risk pools. As healthier enrollees leave the marketplace due to higher out-of-pocket costs after the subsidy expiration, it leaves behind a smaller, higher-risk and sicker pool of members, forcing premiums up for those who remain.
“Insurance is the spread of risk,” said Tana Howard, Wyoming Department of Insurance deputy commissioner. “The more you spread the risk, the less risk there is. With a smaller risk pool, more risk exists. Now there are lots more factors related to risk than just the pool size, such as age, occupation, area, health, etc. Any time risk is increased, generally the price is, too.”
According to KFF, a nonpartisan health policy organization, four percentage points of the increase is directly attributable to this dynamic.
Wyoming is one of many states that saw a steep drop in the number of people who purchased health insurance through the marketplace. Federal data shows that from February 2025 to February 2026, Johnson County experienced an 11% decline — or 117 people — in the number of individuals who purchased insurance through the marketplace. Statewide, 22% fewer, or about 9,800 people, purchased insurance through the marketplace.
In its February 2026 policy update, the Wyoming Hospital Association warned that between 11,000 and 20,000 Wyoming residents were projected to lose health coverage due to the expiration of enhanced subsidies. As more residents become uninsured, hospitals and emergency departments absorb higher levels of uncompensated care for patients who cannot pay out-of-pocket.
“It hurt with those subsidies going away because it really helped a lot of our self-employed individuals in Wyoming, like farmers, ranchers, who were over the 400%,” Howard said. “It allowed them to get those subsidies, and I think that’s really who we’re seeing drop off last year with losing those subsidies.”
KFF anticipates that the number of people who enroll will continue to decline through 2027.
The premium increases will mostly affect people who make above 400% of the poverty rate and don’t qualify for subsidies. That’s a household income of just under $64,000 for singles or $132,480 for a family of four, which is about 13% of enrollees.
For example, a Johnson County couple in their early 60s with a household income of $80,000 would qualify for an estimated $4,736 in monthly premium tax credits, covering about 87% of the cost of their monthly premiums, according to a price estimator calculator from KFF. That would reduce their monthly premium to approximately $681, or $8,176 annually — about 10.2% of their household income for a silver-level health insurance plan through the Affordable Care Act Marketplace in 2027.
However, if that same couple earned $87,000 or more annually, pushing their income above 400% of the poverty rate, they would not qualify for any subsidies. They would face an estimated monthly premium of $5,418, or $65,011 annually — about 75% of their annual income — for a silver- level health insurance plan through the marketplace in 2027.
Other options
Howard said that in the past year, the Department of Insurance has seen consumers switch to private insurance plans that are not qualified health plans. There are a variety of private plans available: Some cover certain medical conditions, such as cancer; others cover accidents only; others cover catastrophic health events, like heart attacks; some only cover hospital stays.
“There’s a lot of different types of health insurance plans,” she said. “There are a lot of different supplemental plans that can fill everybody’s different needs. Insurance isn’t one size fits all. Everybody’s different. There’s a lot of different plans for everybody with different needs.”
But the thing that the private plans have in common is that the plans do not have to offer the same benefits as a qualified health plan sold on the ACA Marketplace. Qualified health plans cover a broad range of essential health benefits (like doctor visits, hospital stays, emergency care and prescription drugs) and have a legal limit on how much a person has to pay out of pocket each year.
“We are seeing a little bit of increase, like in different inquiries and complaints about other types of insurance that they got,” Howard said. “You know, thinking that they were covered and maybe something isn’t covered. They try to do the right thing. They try to find the right insurance, and sometimes they find a good solution, and others maybe aren’t as good as others.”
Howard said healthcare sharing ministries are increasingly popular in Wyoming, but she said those ministries are not insurance and therefore are not subject to any regulation by the State Department of Insurance.
“Healthcare sharing ministries are a whole different beast,” she said. “Because it’s not insurance, that means those plans are not reviewed. Healthcare sharing ministries are not licensed.”
Howard said that there are more than 1,200 licensed insurers in the state. Among other things, the licensing procedure looks at an insurer’s financials to ensure that the insurer is financially stable and can pay claims. Healthcare sharing ministries are not subject to the same financial scrutiny.
“They’re very popular in Wyoming, to our understanding,” she said. “We don’t have any data to support that because they don’t report to us. But we know that they exist, and we know that they’re out there.”
Buyer beware
“You really have to be careful, especially during open enrollment,” Howard said. “I can’t stress enough that people verify the insurance company, make sure they’re licensed. Getting things in writing and seeing that fine print really is important these days.”
Howard said insurance scams are increasingly prevalent, and her office receives complaints weekly.
“I can’t tell you how many complaints we get on a weekly basis for people who bought insurance over the phone and never saw anything in writing,” she said. “People are unfortunately taking advantage of individuals who are trying to do the right thing and protect their family, and it’s hard to watch.”
If consumers do find themselves in a situation, Howard advised that they contact the Department of Insurance to file a complaint.
“We can help them resolve that and try to be an advocate for them to get those claims paid,” she said. “That also helps us find the bad actors.”
Wyomingites can renew, review or explore new health coverage on the Health Insurance Marketplace between Nov. 1 and Jan. 15 during open enrollment, according to Enroll Wyoming. Individuals who enroll by Dec. 15 will have coverage effective Jan. 1, 2027.
