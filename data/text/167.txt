Parting thoughts: Lawrence Wolfe, only serving one year, reflects on his time on Cheyenne City Council
CHEYENNE, Wyo. — Cheyenne City councilors, the publicly elected decision-makers for all the most important local matters, typically serve four-year terms. That was not the case for Ward I Councilmember Lawrence Wolfe, who joined the council under unique circumstances and had only a year to leave his mark. Choosing to let a new councilmember fill his seat, he reflected on briefly having a vote on some of the most consequential moments in the city’s modern history.
The career lawyer wasn’t actually elected to the position.
He was selected
from a handful of candidates by the council after
the sudden death of Cheyenne City Councilmember Scott Roybal
, who had just a year left on his term when he passed in September 2025. Choosing not to run for another term, Wolfe was left with just Roybal’s last year on the council. Even the moment Wolfe was sworn onto the council was unusual.
“I actually went on a trip to see my daughter, who lives in Paris, France. I was visiting her when the final decision was made and then I got sworn in over the phone,” Wolfe said.
Wolfe didn’t have many expectations for his tenure on the council at the time, he said. The energized new councilor, who worked for 45 years as a lawyer and sat on a number of local boards throughout his career, set a list of goals for himself — most of which he would learn were impossible to achieve without motivating the entire city to help.
One was a citywide strategic plan. PlanCheyenne only establishes a long-term vision for the city’s infrastructure planning and land development, meaning Cheyenne has no comprehensive city strategic plan, Wolfe said. He also petitioned for the city to strategize a full citywide AI implementation. Just like individual strategic plans, he continued, different departments use AI differently. He said he wanted to find a way to integrate AI into city operations and train city personnel on using AI tools to improve efficiency in a consistent way.
“Those were kind of the two things that were on my plan,” Wolfe said. “But, unless you can get buy-in from the people who are actually running the city, one city councilperson can’t accomplish that.”
Originally frustrated and disheartened by his inability to get anything done, he learned early on from his fellow councilors that there was no set expectation for what he was actually supposed to do besides representing his ward during votes.
Cheyenne is a strong-mayor city, meaning Cheyenne Mayor Patrick Collins and his staff run the city more than the council does. Councilors can make recommendations and request that city staff assist with drafting resolutions and ordinances, but it is the mayor who ultimately sets the direction for the city government. Councilors are responsible for considering the public impact of changes the city wants to make and casting their votes on behalf of their constituents.
“You’re a free agent,” Wolfe said.
No one is taking attendance, and it’s up to the individual to make the job what they want it to be. There have been individuals on the council who dedicate an excessive amount of time to the job, doing their best to make as large of a splash as possible; there have also historically been councilors who have treated the job as the part-time gig that it technically is.
“The inclination that I think people have when they first get on the council is, ‘Well, I’ve got to go fix things. There are clearly things here that aren’t working very well. It’s my job to go fix them.’ I thought that for a little while and then people kept telling me no. They had told me before I got on that the council doesn’t run the city.”
The fledgling councilor’s mindset shifted dramatically after those first few months. He began focusing on the idea of “scarcity versus abundance” — how he could leverage his vote on the council, which he was given just as the city began to field interest from technology companies like Microsoft and Google wanting to build data centers in town, to best set Cheyenne up for future economic success.
In practice, his approach has appeared somewhat adversarial to business interests. For example,
in June 2026, Wolfe introduced a proposal to enforce a four-month delay to the impending annexation of 3,500 acres for a future Microsoft data center campus and make the company pledge $50 million to the city
. What a majority of his fellow councilors saw as a pay-to-play scheme was, to Wolfe, a bet that companies like Microsoft saw the advantage of setting up shop in Cheyenne as important enough to invest even more into the community, setting it up for greater success down the line.
“So many of the council folks came up in a time when the city didn’t have much money. We had no reserves in 2020. I think the city had $12,000 in total cash reserves. We couldn’t pay our bills more than about a couple weeks in advance,” Wolfe said. “Now we have $55 million in reserves, but almost all the city council folks have come up in a time of what I call a time of scarcity, where just getting new businesses seemed to be the most important thing.”
While others were afraid of losing out on potential investments from data center companies, Wolfe was afraid of giving blank check approval to the corporations and missing out on real community investments. He stepped onto the dais at a moment when the city was already experiencing relative prosperity and poised to attract more major industrial investments.
“When you have a scarcity mentality, you welcome anybody with any ideas, with any kind of investment — particularly with a company like Microsoft, who has been around for a long time and who people think we know and that they’re good corporate citizens and they’ll continue to be good corporate citizens,” Wolfe said. “I spent my life, my career working with big corporations and trying to understand how they work. You’ve got to realize that, when you’re the government, you’ve got to be very skeptical of all of these big corporations, but particularly now and particularly with this. And it’s that level of skepticism that has been missing.”
He also didn’t expect how complicated navigating the many moving parts of municipal government could be. When the city wasn’t making hard decisions regarding data centers, it was considering things like
the WY Fresh annexation
or trying to get the
Reed Avenue Rail Corridor Project back off the ground
. He had to learn about how cities make both the big and little decisions as he went. That reinforced his belief in just how hard the real experts work.
“When you’re on the city council, you really grow to appreciate the things we do really well as a city: pick up the trash, keep the roads clear and clean, build the roads that need to be built,” Wolfe said. “One of the biggest ones is deliver clean water and take the sewage away and do something with it. All of those are parts of the city and these are 24/7 jobs.”
He also learned to have a lot of sympathy for his fellow councilors who have made a longtime commitment to the job. The psychological toll of late-night meetings, during which members of the public often belittled their councilors and called them corrupt, is something he said he hasn’t perfectly adjusted to. His fellow councilors, meanwhile, handle those comments with grace. There isn’t a “black hand” puppeteering the city from the shadows, he said; just a couple of really dedicated public servants trying their best to make the city a better place.
“The mayor and the council are responsive to a wide range of constituents, and there are certain groups like the realtors and the people who are doing construction who we see all the time and we get to know, but I haven’t seen anybody that has an inordinate influence over us from an outside perspective,” he said.
So why is Wolfe stepping down after just a year? For one, he said, it’s always a good time to let the younger people take the reins. The 75-year-old said he has done enough over his long career.
“People my age have an obligation to not do those things, to get out of the way and let people who have their future and their stake in the future make these judgments,” he said.
His Ward I successor, Maren Kallas, is just over half of his age. She will also help round out a Cheyenne City Council with four women councilors, which is the most that have served on a council at one time this century.
“I think the council will benefit from both her youth and the perspective that women bring to groups like this, which is really critical,” Wolfe said. “I have a long, long career of trying to accomplish these kinds of things. I retired at a young age from a very large law firm because people were there and all lined up and they needed to have us get out of the way. She doesn’t have the breadth of knowledge and experience that I have, but she’ll bring other qualities and she’ll learn. I don’t have any concerns about that.”
As for what he plans to do next, Wolfe said he is still interested in community service, just in a different form. Whether that be with Habitat for Humanity or another local organization, he doesn’t yet know. He said he does know, however, that he is going to try to carve out more time for the sweeter parts of retirement, including hopping back on his bike and traveling a bit.
