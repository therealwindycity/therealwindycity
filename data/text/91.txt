Former Wyoming Boys’ School resident suing for alleged abuse told staff they were ‘like family,’ witness testifies
While acknowledging positive aspects of the school, witnesses and attorneys suggest Blaise Chivers-King suffered trauma from his time at the state facility.
by
Wyofile
November 21, 2026
November 20, 2026

CASPER—Blaise Chivers-King, the former Wyoming Boys’ School resident suing school staff for alleged abuse, stands out as a memorable student to the employees who worked with him.
He was the first resident who was quarantined when he arrived at the state facility during the COVID-19 pandemic. The 15-year-old needed a lot of attention. Chivers-King had a history of trauma, and he often struggled to manage his emotions, boys’ school staff testified at the trial spurred by the former resident’s lawsuit. Staff described doing various activities with him like watching movies, designing custom shoes, playing card games, folding origami, looking at World of Warcraft characters and just talking. At one point, Chivers-King wanted to try different kinds of hot sauce, so staff members picked some up for him in town.
“The kid wanted somebody to love him and care about him, and that’s what we tried to do,” Bryan DuPree, a Wyoming Boys’ School supervisor, testified Tuesday.
Chivers-King is one of six former boys’ school residents who sued school staff in 2024. The school, which detains delinquent boys ages 12 to 21, is a 38-acre residential facility near Worland that’s overseen by the Wyoming Department of Family Services.
Chivers-King, who spent time at the boys’ school between 2020 and 2022, alleged staff violated his 14th Amendment rights by placing him in prolonged isolation, using excessive force and infringing on due process. He is suing in part for days he spent
locked in 7-by-10-foot cinderblock rooms
under conditions that his attorneys consider solitary confinement. One of those stints lasted two weeks.
Several employees testified that Chivers-King kept in touch after he left the boys’ school. He talked with them about dogs, boxing, getting a job and visiting a haunted house in Denver. He sent videos of himself doing figure eights on his new motorcycle and pictures of a visit to a zoo.
Their testimony paints a broader picture of how students were treated at the school that adds to depictions offered by other witnesses, who described staff forcing kids to shave their heads, walk in single-file lines, look forward without talking and refrain from communicating with one another. A former boys’ school employee testified that
staff “would make degrading comments to escalate a kid.”
Stuart Grassian, a Massachusetts psychiatrist who testified at the trial as an expert witness, described the school as
having “a culture of total control and domination.”
But the former student’s attorneys and witnesses have acknowledged there were also positive aspects of Chivers-King’s time at the boys’ school. Asked about staff taking Chivers-King to the Humane Society to walk a dog, Grassian, the psychiatrist, commented that he “thought that was terrific,” adding that not everything at the boys’ school is “evil.”
Chivers-King’s attorneys have emphasized that his claims only concern select incidents and time periods. One pointed to a day outside of the claims in which a logbook indicates Chivers-King was let out of the detention room for several hours to watch “Miss Peregrine’s Home for Peculiar Children” and a Marvel movie. Another suggested that just because not everything at the boys’ school was “horrible” doesn’t mean the former resident didn’t suffer trauma from his time there.
While many spoke fondly of Chivers-King, boys’ school employees called to testify in the trial described challenges they faced in managing his needs while keeping the school safe.
Among the witnesses defense attorneys called were two Wyoming Boys’ School employees whom Chivers-King testified about becoming close with during his time at the facility. Neither are defendants in the case.
“A lot of people knew that me and Blaise were tight,” Craig Jones, a boys’ school dorm director, testified. “When Blaise was having a hard time, they asked me a lot of times to come over and help.”
John Schwalbe, a dorm supervisor, said Chivers-King would keep in contact almost on a weekly basis before the lawsuit. “He would tell us that we were like family to him and he missed us.”
During cross examination, Chivers-King’s attorneys probed dynamics that might incentivize the employees to offer testimony that reflects positively on the school, including that they work for the facility and their colleagues are defendants in the case.
Boys’ school staff testified that they continued to interact with Chivers-King frequently even when he was confined in detention rooms at the facility, including going into the room to talk with him and offering him exercise, reading materials, therapy and schoolwork, though they said he often rejected these services.
“I believe that the strict definition of solitary confinement pertaining to Blaise Chivers-King was not met,” Dr. Dominique Sotelo, an associate professor of clinical mental health counseling who worked in juvenile justice for 22 years, testified. The expert witness explained that the services staff were supposed to provide for Chivers-King while he was confined would require them to open the detention room door. Sotelo said that “even the attempt or the approach” to offer things like school would fall “under the definition of meaningful contact.”
But dorm logbooks, which staff use to record their checks of students in isolation, don’t reflect such interactions or that staff regularly offered Chivers-King schoolwork, reading, therapy or exercise. Mike Nelson, a defendant in the case who worked as a risk management officer, a dorm director and a dorm supervisor when Chivers-King was at the school, testified that the logbooks are “incredibly incomplete.” Other employees also testified that they wouldn’t log every interaction they had with Chivers-King while he was locked in a detention room.
“What I saw is the logbooks at times would meet the standards in terms of documenting the detail,” said Sotelo, who used American Corrections Association standards to form his opinion about the boys’ school’s detention practices. He also noted times when there wasn’t very much detail in the logbooks. “You want a good record of what’s going on when someone is placed in isolation,” he said.
Chivers-King’s attorneys have pointed to numerous times when video and school records show that the former resident had calmed down but was not released from a detention room. Records also show that staff expected Chivers-King to demonstrate a “credible” commitment to being safe by having two consecutive days of good behavior in detention before he could be released. This predetermined amount of detention time is central to attorneys’ argument that staff violated the 14th Amendment, which protects detained people from arbitrary punishment.
