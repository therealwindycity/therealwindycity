Cheyenne passes new urban farm rules, throws out pet, nuisance exemptions for farmers

CHEYENNE, Wyo. — The Cheyenne City Council has successfully passed an ordinance establishing new rules for urban farming,
a long-time effort spurred by the annexation of the WY Fresh farm earlier this month
. With the new rules in place, Cheyenne residents can keep animals on lots of certain sizes. A pair of ordinances designed to make the farm life even more tenable for city residents, however, failed to garner enough council support.
The Cheyenne government has been looking at adopting new Unified Development Code rules pertaining to agriculture for months. The move aligned with the planned annexation of the WY Fresh Farm, a local farm stand selling agricultural products out of its property south of Cheyenne. Originally in the county, the property was completely surrounded by city land. Cheyenne has been trying to close up those holes of county land for years but, without clear city rules pertaining to agriculture, the annexation would have doomed the business.
The property has since been annexed and now, after a final round of council votes Monday, can continue operating similarly to how it did when it was in the county.
The property’s owners, however, told the city ahead of the annexation that they were planning on selling the property.
The rules will apply to any property in the city with one of the proper zoning designations, including Agriculture, Public, Light Industrial, Heavy Industrial and Airport District. Some other zones allow agriculture with special city permission.
Those properties can keep animals as small as rabbits or as large as cattle so long as they maintain a specific animal-to-acreage ratio. Animals are quantified via “Animal Units.” A cow or a horse is a single Animal Unit, while smaller livestock like rabbits and chickens are only a fraction of one. Per the ordinance, property owners can keep one Animal Unit per acre of land they own.
Every city zone, meanwhile, will be able to engage in gardens and homemade foods, a new land use definition established by the newly passed changes. Per the UDC, property owners can maintain a garden or sell their own homemade agricultural products wherever they want as long as they don’t make more than $10,000 each year in gross revenue.
The ordinance was passed via substitute, with the substitute adding a grandfathered status to farms that were originally compliant before being forcibly annexed, protecting them from immediately being in violation of city code when they are incorporated into city limits.
Two additional companion ordinances that would have expanded new land use exemptions for urban farmers were rejected on second reading. One would have exempted them from the city’s rules on animals like dogs and cats, allowing them to keep more than the city-mandated maximum of four. The second would have exempted them from being responsible for maintaining right-of-ways near their property line, permitting them to skip mowing, clearing snow or removing trash between their property line and the road.
The original motivation for animal limit exemption for farm properties was that working dogs and cats serve a special purpose on a farm and aren’t simply pets. They chase rodents, protect livestock and keep the farm safe. Not only would the change have allowed farms to keep the animals, but livestock guardian dogs would have also been exempt from nuisance rules regarding barking.
The owners of WY Fresh said that, without the ordinance being passed, they became code violators overnight when the annexation of their property was approved.
“I had protections in the county that need to continue now that I’m part of the city,” Tommie Kniseley, co-owner of WY Fresh, said.
However, with councilors unconvinced that guardian dogs should be exempt from noise complaints
and noting that state statute already legally protects working dogs
, the majority voted down the ordinance. The ordinance exempting urban farmers from looking after nearby right-of-ways was struck down for a similar reason: Laramie County required property owners to look after right-of-ways, so urban farmers won’t be doing anything they weren’t already supposed to be doing.
Being voted down, both ordinances would need to be reworked and introduced again in the future to be considered.
